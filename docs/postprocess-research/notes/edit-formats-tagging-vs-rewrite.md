# Tagging and edit-list output vs full rewrites

Research agent report, 2026-09-25 (web sources as cited; claims marked unverified are as the agent flagged them).

## Research findings: tag-don't-rewrite and edit-list architectures for text correction

### 1. GECToR — "GECToR – Grammatical Error Correction: Tag, Not Rewrite"
- **Paper**: Omelianchuk, Atrasevych, Chernodub, Skurzhanskyi (Grammarly), ACL 2020 BEA Workshop. arXiv:2005.12592 — https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.12592
- **Core idea**: Cast GEC as sequence tagging — a BERT-style encoder predicts a token-level edit tag (keep/delete/replace/append/custom transform) for every input token in one or a few non-autoregressive passes, instead of decoding a full rewritten sentence.
- **Speedup**: Abstract states "up to 10 times as fast as a Transformer-based seq2seq GEC system." Grammarly's own engineering blog gives concrete latency numbers: Transformer-NMT beam=12 → 4.35s; beam=1 → 0.71s; GECToR 5 iterations → 0.40s; GECToR 1 iteration → 0.20s. https://www.grammarly.com/blog/engineering/gec-tag-not-rewrite/
- **Accuracy**: F0.5 = 65.3 (single)/66.5 (ensemble) on CoNLL-2014; 72.4/73.6 on BEA-2019 (from the paper). **Tradeoff**: the paper/blog is explicit that cutting the iteration count "speeds up the overall pipeline while trading off the quality of the corrections" — i.e., the speed/accuracy tradeoff is tunable via iteration count, not a fixed penalty vs. seq2seq.

### 2. Seq2Edits
- **Paper**: Stahlberg & Kumar (Google), EMNLP 2020. arXiv:2009.11136 — https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.11136 (also https://aclanthology.org/2020.emnlp-main.418/)
- **Core idea**: Represent a seq2seq transduction as a sequence of span-level edit operations (replace-this-span-with-these-tokens, or keep-unchanged), still generated autoregressively, but output length now scales with number of edits rather than target length.
- **Speedup**: "speeds up inference by up to 5.2x compared to full sequence models" for GEC, because inference time depends on edit count, not token count (confirmed on abstract page).
- **Accuracy**: Abstract reports "competitive results" across five tasks (text normalization, sentence fusion, splitting/rephrasing, simplification, GEC) but **does not give specific F0.5/BLEU numbers in the abstract** — not found precise deltas vs. seq2seq baseline without reading the full paper tables.

### 3. EdiT5
- **Paper**: Mallinson, Adamek, Malmi, Severyn (Google), Findings of EMNLP 2022. arXiv:2205.12209 — https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.12209 (also https://aclanthology.org/2022.findings-emnlp.156/)
- **Core idea**: Semi-autoregressive — tagging and re-ordering are non-autoregressive (bulk of the output), while only genuinely new inserted spans go through a short autoregressive decoder; the model is warm-started from a T5 checkpoint.
- **Speedup**: "speedups of up to 25x when compared to seq2seq models," since most tasks need far fewer autoregressive steps (confirmed on abstract page).
- **Accuracy**: "comparable performance to T5" in high-resource settings, and "clearly outperforming T5" in low-resource settings, across Sentence Fusion, GEC, and Decontextualization — i.e., **no reported accuracy penalty**, and a net win when training data is scarce.

### 4. LaserTagger (Malmi et al., 2019)
- **Paper**: "Encode, Tag, Realize: High-Precision Text Editing" — Malmi, Krause, Rothe, Mirylenka, Severyn (Google), EMNLP-IJCNLP 2019. arXiv:1909.01187 — https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.01187 (also https://aclanthology.org/D19-1510/, code: https://github.com/google-research/lasertagger)
- **Core idea**: Predict three per-token edit operations (keep / delete / add-phrase-from-a-fixed-vocabulary) via a BERT encoder + small autoregressive decoder restricted to a bounded phrase vocabulary, rather than free-form generation over the full vocabulary.
- **Speedup**: Abstract states tagging "can be more than two orders of magnitude faster" (>100x) than comparable seq2seq models at inference time.
- **Accuracy**: SOTA on 3 of 4 evaluated tasks (sentence fusion, splitting, summarization; grammar correction included as the 4th); "performs comparably to a set of strong seq2seq baselines with a large number of training examples, and outperforms them when the number of examples is limited" — again no penalty reported, favorable in low-resource regimes.

### 5. FELIX (decoder-free text editing)
- **Paper**: Mallinson, Severyn, Malmi, Garrido (Google), Findings of EMNLP 2020. arXiv:2003.10687 — https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.10687 (also https://aclanthology.org/2020.findings-emnlp.111/, Google blog: https://research.google/blog/introducing-felix-flexible-text-editing-through-tagging-and-insertion/)
- **Core idea**: Fully non-autoregressive/decoder-free — a Pointer network handles tagging (choosing/reordering the subset of input tokens to keep) and a Masked Language Model handles insertion (in-filling missing tokens in parallel); no autoregressive decoder at all.
- **Speedup**: **Not found.** I checked both the abstract and the full text (via https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2003.10687) — the paper only claims speed qualitatively ("fast at inference time," non-autoregressive "to guarantee faster inference") and reports one absolute number (a beam-search pointing-mechanism overhead of "about 12ms... on a Xeon CPU@3.7GHz"), not a comparative "Nx faster" figure vs. any seq2seq baseline. Treat any "90x"-style number you may see elsewhere for FELIX as unverified — I could not source it.
- **Accuracy tradeoff**: The paper does show FELIX losing to a seq2seq (BERT2BERT) baseline in some full-data settings: on DiscoFuse sentence fusion (100% data), BERT2BERT scores SARI 89.52 / Exact 63.90 vs. FELIX's 88.78 / 61.31; on WMT17 automatic post-editing, a specialized SOTA system (Lee et al. 2019) beats FELIX on TER (18.13 vs. 21.87, lower is better). So FELIX's own paper documents an accuracy cost in high-resource, non-specialized comparisons, in exchange for being decoder-free.

### 6. LLM emitting a compact edit-list/diff instead of a full rewrite
This is indeed a thinner, more scattered literature, as expected — but I found more direct evidence than "nothing published," spanning ASR-transcript editing (most relevant to this app), minimal-edit GEC prompting, and code/structured-data editing (most mature evidence base, different domain).

**Directly on-point — ASR/transcript editing:**
- **NLE: Non-autoregressive LLM-based ASR by Transcript Editing**, arXiv:2603.08397 — https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.08397. Core idea: reformulate ASR as an LLM-based editor that refines an initial ASR hypothesis in parallel rather than regenerating the transcript autoregressively (uses "an interleaved padding strategy [that] exploits the identity mapping bias of Transformers, allowing the model to focus on corrections rather than full reconstruction"). Reported: **27x speedup over the AR baseline** (single-utterance), RTFx of 1630, and 5.67% average WER on the Open ASR leaderboard (NLE++ variant). Flag: this is a very recent 2026 preprint I could not cross-verify against independent reviews or confirm peer-review status — treat the specific numbers as single-source.
- **FastCorrect: Fast Error Correction with Edit Alignment for Automatic Speech Recognition** (Microsoft, NeurIPS 2021), arXiv:2105.03842 — https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.03842v1. Not an LLM (a dedicated small non-autoregressive transducer), but direct precedent for this app's exact task: edit-alignment-based non-autoregressive ASR correction. Reported 6-9x faster inference than autoregressive correction models, with 8-14% WER reduction. Included because it's the closest architectural ancestor to "tag, don't rewrite" applied specifically to ASR transcripts.

**Minimal-edit GEC with prompted LLMs:**
- **"Larger Context Window, Fewer Overcorrections: Optimizing Prompts and Batching for Minimal-Edit Grammatical Error Correction,"** arXiv:2609.10810 (EMNLP 2026 Findings) — https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.10810. Uses Gemini 3.1-Pro prompted (not fine-tuned). Key finding directly relevant to your "70% of the time nothing changes" observation: **prompted LLMs systematically overcorrect** — they rewrite well-formed spans unless mitigated by taxonomy-based instructions and by batching multiple sentences into one context window (an "attention dilution effect" that reduces the edit rate). Achieves F0.5 = 78.32 on BEA-2019, closing the gap to fine-tuned models to 0.38 points. **No latency/speedup numbers reported** — this paper is entirely about accuracy/overcorrection, not speed.

**Code/structured-data editing (same architectural question, different domain, much more evidence):**
- **"To Diff or Not to Diff? Structure-Aware and Adaptive Output Formats for Efficient LLM-based Code Editing,"** arXiv:2604.27296, accepted ACL 2026 Findings — https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.27296, https://aclanthology.org/2026.findings-acl.1483/. Introduces BlockDiff/FuncDiff (structure-aware diff formats) and AdaEdit (learned chooser between diff and full-file output). Result: **matches full-code-generation accuracy while cutting latency and cost by over 30%** on long-code editing tasks.
- **"JSON Whisperer: Efficient JSON Editing with LLMs,"** arXiv:2510.04717 — https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.04717. LLM emits RFC 6902 JSON-Patch diffs instead of the full document. Result: **31% token reduction, edit quality within 5% of full regeneration**.
- **Aider (open-source LLM coding agent) engineering docs** — https://aider.chat/docs/unified-diffs.html: for GPT-4 Turbo, switching from SEARCH/REPLACE to a unified-diff output format raised a refactoring benchmark score from 20% to 61%, and cut "lazy"/incomplete edits from 12 to 4 (out of 89 tasks) — an accuracy/completeness gain from diff-style output, not purely a speed claim. Related page https://aider.chat/docs/more/edit-formats.html confirms diff/udiff formats are used specifically because "the model only needs to return parts of the file which have changes" (token/cost savings) but gives **no exact token-count multiplier** on that page — a "10x fewer tokens, sub-second" figure surfaced in one search-engine summary but I could not confirm it on Aider's own pages, so treat that specific number as unverified.
- **Cautionary/negative result** — "Don't Let the Model Write the YAML: Deterministic, Minimal-Diff GitOps Remediation from LLM-Proposed Field Changes," arXiv:2609.00227 — https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.00227. Found that LLM-emitted unified diffs are unsafe under strict patch application (almost none apply cleanly); a tolerant patcher (GNU patch) applies 96% of them but silently misapplies 14-20% with no error signal; full-file rewrites from frontier models are usually correct but non-deterministic (silently drop a field or edit a neighbor on some runs) and always cost O(file size) per edit. This is a relevant caution for anyone assuming "emit a diff" is automatically safe or cheap — format choice doesn't guarantee correctness.
- **"Copy-as-Decode: Grammar-Constrained Parallel Prefill for LLM Editing,"** arXiv:2604.18170 — https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.18170. Conceptually close to what you're asking (decodes edits as a 2-primitive grammar: `<copy lines="i-j"/>` + `<gen>...</gen>`, verified via constrained decoding; claims 6.8x-303x kernel-level speedup for copying spans via parallel prefill, pooled wall-clock bound ~13x). **Flag: the arXiv page itself states this paper "has been withdrawn by the author following internal review."** I'm citing it because it's directly on-topic, but do not treat its numbers as validated — they were withdrawn, not merely superseded.

**Small dedicated "edit-operation" models beating big LLMs (adjacent, not literally "LLM emits a diff," but same latency motivation):**
- **G-SPEED: General SParse Efficient Editing MoDel**, EMNLP 2023 Findings, arXiv:2310.10480 — https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.10480. A 508M-parameter model trained to predict sparse edit operations. Reported throughput: 754.33 tok/s for G-SPEED vs. 21.05 tok/s for LLaMA-7B (~35.8x) and 39.46 tok/s for T0-3B, while scoring higher on SARI on the WNC benchmark (60.2 vs. 54.1 for LLaMA-7B). Supports the general thesis but is a purpose-built tagging architecture like GECToR, not a general LLM instructed to output diffs.

**Speculative decoding specifically for minimal edits**: I found no paper that quantifies speculative-decoding speedup specifically for grammar-correction or transcript-cleanup minimal-edit workloads. What's published is general: lossless speculative decoding (Leviathan et al. 2023; Chen et al. 2023) and input-derived-draft variants like "Prompt Lookup Decoding" are noted as particularly effective for editing tasks because most output tokens are copied verbatim from the input (high draft-acceptance rate) — this shows up in the code-editing literature (e.g., referenced qualitatively in searches around Aider/code-agent tooling) but I did not find a dedicated benchmark number for prose/transcript editing. Generic (non-editing) reported speedups I found were modest (~1.6x, 15.78s vs. 25.09s in one comparison) — not editing-specific, so **not a reliable proxy** for your use case.

**Adjacent work that does NOT answer the question (still generates full text, not a diff) — included for completeness since you may want to rule it out explicitly:**
- **CoEdIT** (Grammarly), arXiv:2305.09857 — https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.09857, blog: https://www.grammarly.com/blog/engineering/coedit-text-editing/. Instruction-tuned seq2seq model, "nearly 60x smaller than the largest-sized instruction-trained LLMs," but still emits the full rewritten text.
- **PEER** (Meta), arXiv:2208.11663 — https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.11663. Collaborative writing LM trained on Wikipedia edit histories; plans/edits/explains iteratively but still generates full text spans, not a positional diff.

**Bottom line for item 6**: nothing found is a general-purpose LLM prompted or fine-tuned to emit a compact "replace X with Y at position N" or unified-diff patch specifically for prose grammar/transcript correction with a published latency number — the closest direct match is the ASR-specific NLE paper (27x, arXiv:2603.08397) and the minimal-edit-GEC overcorrection study (arXiv:2609.10810, accuracy-only). The strongest quantified evidence for the general "diff output beats full rewrite" claim comes from the code-editing and JSON-editing literature (30%+ latency/cost reduction, 31% token reduction), which is a different domain than prose but the same architectural bet.
